## Core Principle

For nonfiction, preserve the difference between:

- what the author claims;
- what evidence the author uses;
- what assumptions connect evidence to claim;
- what counterarguments or rival explanations exist;
- what is descriptive, causal, normative, speculative, or practical advice.

Do not turn a nonfiction book into generic takeaways. A useful skill should help agents reconstruct the author's argument, apply it within limits, critique it, and locate source support.

## Argumentative Nonfiction

For each chapter or part, extract:

- central claim;
- subclaims and dependencies;
- evidence type: data, anecdote, case, historical episode, experiment, authority, analogy, textual reading;
- assumptions and definitions;
- counterarguments addressed or ignored;
- scope conditions and limits;
- practical implications if the book has them.

Use an `argument-map.md` when future users may ask what the book argues or whether the argument is strong.

## History And Biography

For history, biography, or memoir-as-evidence, extract:

- timeline and periodization;
- key actors, institutions, places, and relationships;
- causal claims and alternative explanations;
- primary vs secondary evidence when visible;
- author standpoint, retrospective framing, and possible self-justification;
- contested facts, uncertain chronology, and source gaps.

For memoir with literary value, also read [literary-distillation.md](literary-distillation.md) and preserve voice, scene construction, and ambiguity.

## Philosophy And Theory

For philosophy, theory, and conceptual humanities work, extract:

- terms of art and definitions;
- distinctions the author needs the reader to maintain;
- argument sequence;
- examples and thought experiments;
- objections, replies, and unresolved problems;
- relation to prior thinkers, schools, or debates;
- misuse risks when applying the theory outside its context.

Use a `concept-map.md` when terminology drives the skill more than chronology.

## Case-Based Books

For business, policy, social science, or popular science books built around cases:

- separate case facts from the author's lesson;
- record why the case was selected if the author says so;
- track whether cases are representative, exceptional, anecdotal, or illustrative;
- preserve failed cases, contrary evidence, and base-rate concerns;
- avoid promoting the author's lesson as universal unless the book provides support.

## Quality Rules

- Separate author claim, evidence, inference, and your interpretation.
- Mark normative claims as normative. Do not present them as facts.
- Mark causal claims and the evidence used for causality.
- Do not let memorable anecdotes substitute for evidence.
- Preserve counterarguments and missing counterarguments.
- Track dates, names, places, and cited works carefully.
- Escalate to raw pages for exact definitions, quotes, dates, statistics, and cited source titles.
- When a claim depends on current facts, use external authoritative sources before treating it as current.
